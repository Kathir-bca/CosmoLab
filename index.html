🌌 Big Bang

The Big Bang is the leading cosmological model describing the evolution of the universe from an extremely hot, dense early state to the expanding, structured universe we observe today. It is not best understood as an ordinary explosion at one location in pre-existing space.

🎬 Video

Original video hosted by YouTube/National Geographic. CosmoLab embeds the original video rather than copying or re-uploading it.

📖 What is it?

The Big Bang model describes a universe that was much hotter and denser in the past. As the universe expanded, its temperature and density decreased. This changing environment allowed new physical processes to occur, from the formation of light nuclei to atoms, stars, galaxies, and larger cosmic structures.

The age of the universe is approximately 13.8 billion years. The model describes the universe's evolution extremely successfully, but it does not give us a complete physical description of an absolute beginning. In particular, treating a mathematical singularity from classical general relativity as a literal physical point would go beyond what current physics can establish.

⚙️ How does it work?

Very early universe

Many modern cosmological models include an extremely rapid early expansion called cosmic inflation. Its physical mechanism remains unknown.

Hot early universe

After the inflationary phase, the universe was an extremely hot environment containing radiation and particles. Expansion caused it to cool.

First minutes

Nuclear reactions produced light nuclei, primarily hydrogen and helium, with small amounts of other light nuclei.

About 380,000 years

Electrons combined with nuclei to form mostly neutral atoms. The universe became transparent and photons could travel freely. That ancient radiation is observed today as the CMB.

❓ Why does it matter?

The Big Bang model connects several independent observations into one coherent history. It explains why the universe was hotter and denser in the past, why cosmic background radiation exists, why the early universe produced the observed light elements, and how small early density variations could grow into the large-scale structure of galaxies and clusters.

🔬 Evidence

🌠 Cosmic expansion

Distant galaxies show systematic redshifts associated with cosmic expansion. Looking backward in an expanding universe leads to a hotter and denser past.

📡 Cosmic Microwave Background

The CMB is relic radiation from when the universe became transparent about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Its near-uniformity and tiny fluctuations provide detailed information about the early universe.

⚛️ Light-element abundances

Big Bang nucleosynthesis predicts the production of the light nuclei, especially hydrogen and helium, in the hot early universe. Observed primordial abundances provide an important test.

🌌 Large-scale structure

Tiny density variations visible in the CMB provide seeds that gravity could amplify into galaxies, clusters, and the cosmic web.

⏳ Timeline / development

Very early → Inflation

The earliest period is not completely understood. Inflation is a leading framework for describing an extraordinarily rapid expansion phase.

First minutes

Nuclear reactions produced the first light nuclei as the universe expanded and cooled.

380,000 years

Recombination made the universe transparent, allowing the CMB photons to travel freely.

First stars and galaxies

Gravity amplified matter-density differences, eventually producing the first stars and galaxies.

Today

The universe is still expanding, and observations show that the expansion is accelerating.

❓ Common questions

Was the Big Bang an explosion?

No. The model describes the expansion and evolution of the universe itself, rather than an explosion from one location into pre-existing space.

Where did the Big Bang happen?

The standard cosmological picture does not identify a special center from which the universe expanded. Expansion occurs throughout space.

What happened before the Big Bang?

There is no confirmed answer. Some theoretical models explore earlier phases, but current observations do not establish one as fact.

Did everything begin as an infinitely small point?

Not as an established physical fact. A singularity can appear when classical general relativity is extrapolated to extreme conditions, but our current theories are incomplete there.

Is the universe still expanding?

Yes. Observations show cosmic expansion, and the expansion is currently accelerating.

What is the universe expanding into?

The standard model does not require an external space for the universe to expand into. The distances between sufficiently separated objects increase as the geometry of space evolves.

How do we know the Big Bang happened?

No single observation proves the whole model. The case comes from multiple independent observations, especially cosmic expansion, the CMB, primordial light-element abundances, and large-scale structure.

💨 Cosmic Inflation

Cosmic inflation is a hypothesis that the very early universe underwent a period of accelerated expansion before the standard radiation-dominated evolution of the hot Big Bang. It was proposed to explain the universe's large-scale uniformity, near-flat geometry, and the origin of primordial fluctuations that later helped form cosmic structure.

Scientific status: Inflation is a leading framework in modern cosmology and its broad predictions are compatible with observations, but no single inflation model has been uniquely established and primordial gravitational waves have not been conclusively detected.

🎬 Video

Original video is embedded through YouTube; CosmoLab does not download or re-upload it.

📖 What is it?

Inflation proposes that, extremely early in cosmic history, the scale factor of the universe underwent accelerated expansion, ä > 0. In many models the expansion is approximately exponential for a limited period, but realistic inflation is generally quasi-exponential rather than perfectly exponential.

Inflation is not the Big Bang itself and does not establish an absolute beginning of time or the universe. It is a proposed phase in cosmic evolution.

⚙️ How does it work?

1. Accelerated expansion

In general relativity, sufficiently negative pressure can produce accelerated expansion. The condition is p < −ρ/3.

2. Inflaton

Many models use a scalar field called the inflaton. Its potential energy can drive accelerated expansion. Its physical identity has not been experimentally established.

3. Quantum fluctuations

Tiny quantum fluctuations can be stretched to cosmological scales. They become primordial perturbations that later provide seeds for structure formation.

4. Reheating

When inflation ends, inflationary energy is transferred into particles and radiation, producing the hot thermal conditions used by standard Big Bang cosmology.

🎯 Why was inflation proposed?

🌌 Horizon problem

Inflation provides a mechanism in which regions that were once able to interact can become enormously separated by later expansion.

📐 Flatness problem

Accelerated expansion strongly suppresses the relative importance of spatial curvature, driving the geometry toward flatness.

🌱 Structure

Quantum fluctuations stretched during inflation can supply the primordial density variations later amplified by gravity into galaxies and the cosmic web.

🧩 Initial conditions

Inflation is one proposed framework for generating suitable conditions for the subsequent hot Big Bang evolution.

📐 Mathematics

Hubble parameter

H = ȧ/a

H measures the fractional expansion rate of the scale factor a(t).

Inflation condition

ä > 0

The scale factor accelerates during inflation.

Scalar-field energy

ρ = ½φ̇² + V(φ)

A canonical scalar field has kinetic and potential contributions to its energy density.

E-folds

N = ln(aend/astart)

E-folds measure how much the scale factor grew rather than describing inflation only by elapsed time.

🔬 Evidence, predictions & limits

CMB measurements show primordial fluctuations with properties broadly compatible with many inflationary models, including an approximately scale-invariant spectrum. The universe is also observed to be very close to spatially flat.

Inflationary models can predict primordial gravitational waves. Their imprint could appear in CMB polarization, especially B-modes. Current observations provide upper limits on such a primordial signal rather than a conclusive detection.

What remains unknown

The identity of the inflaton, its potential, the detailed reheating process, and whether inflation was the earliest phase are unknown.

No unique model

Different inflationary models can produce similar broad predictions, so observations have not selected one unique model.

The inflationary phase ends and its energy is transferred into particles and radiation through reheating.

The universe continues through the hot expanding history that includes nucleosynthesis, recombination, the CMB, stars and galaxies.

❓ Common questions

Did inflation happen before the Big Bang?

In many modern models, inflation precedes the standard hot, radiation-dominated Big Bang evolution. The wording depends on how “Big Bang” is defined.

Did inflation make objects travel faster than light?

No. Local objects do not need to exceed c. Inflation concerns the rapid expansion of spacetime distances.

What was inflating?

Many models use an inflaton scalar field, but no experimentally confirmed inflaton has been identified.

How long did inflation last?

There is no model-independent measured duration. Quoted timescales are model-dependent.

Did inflation explain the absolute beginning?

No. Inflation is a proposed early phase and does not by itself determine whether the universe had an absolute beginning.

🌠 Expansion of Space

The expansion of space means that, on sufficiently large cosmic scales, the distances between galaxies that are carried along with the expanding universe increase with time. It is not an explosion from a central point: the geometry of space itself evolves, so there is no known special center of expansion.

Scientific status: Cosmic expansion is strongly supported by observations. The present expansion is also accelerating, although the physical nature of dark energy—the name given to whatever causes that acceleration—remains unknown.

🎬 Video

Original video is embedded through YouTube; CosmoLab does not download or re-upload it.

📖 What is it?

In an expanding universe, the large-scale distance between galaxies changes because the scale of the universe changes. A useful mathematical description introduces a scale factor, a(t), which tells us how cosmic distances change relative to a chosen reference time.

Galaxies are not necessarily flying through a pre-existing empty space away from one central explosion. On very large scales, the space between objects grows. This is why an observer in another sufficiently typical galaxy also sees distant galaxies receding.

⚙️ How does it work?

1. Scale factor

If the scale factor increases, the proper distance between sufficiently separated comoving galaxies increases. The scale factor is a description of the evolving geometry of the universe.

2. Hubble parameter

H(t) = ȧ/a

The Hubble parameter measures the fractional expansion rate at a particular cosmic time. Its present value is called the Hubble constant, H₀.

3. Hubble–Lemaître relation

v ≈ H₀d

For sufficiently nearby galaxies, recession velocity is approximately proportional to distance. At very large distances, relativistic cosmology is needed rather than treating this as an ordinary velocity through space.

4. Light is stretched

1 + z = a₀/aemit

As light travels through expanding space, its wavelength is stretched. This produces cosmological redshift, allowing astronomers to study the expansion history.

🔭 How do we know?

🔴 Galaxy redshifts

Spectral features from distant galaxies are shifted toward longer wavelengths. The pattern of redshift with distance is consistent with an expanding universe.

📏 Distance measurements

Astronomers combine distance indicators such as Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae with redshift measurements to reconstruct how the expansion changes over time.

💥 Type Ia supernovae

Distant Type Ia supernovae helped reveal that cosmic expansion has been accelerating rather than simply slowing under gravity.

📡 Cosmic Microwave Background

The CMB provides an early-universe snapshot. Comparing its information with later observations allows scientists to constrain the universe's expansion history and cosmological parameters.

🧭 What is actually expanding?

The expansion is a change in the large-scale geometry of spacetime. It does not mean that every object, atom, planet, or galaxy is being stretched in the same way. Local structures held together by gravity or other forces can remain bound while the universe expands on much larger scales.

Space between distant galaxies grows Atoms do not expand with the universe Solar systems remain gravitationally bound Galaxy groups can remain bound

📈 Why is expansion accelerating?

Measurements of distant Type Ia supernovae showed that cosmic expansion has been accelerating over the later part of cosmic history. In the standard cosmological model, this behavior is described using dark energy, but dark energy is a label for the unknown cause or component responsible for the observed acceleration, not a complete explanation of its nature.

Current observations therefore distinguish two facts: the expansion is observed, while the physical nature of the accelerated-expansion component is still an open question.

This gives the fractional rate of change of the scale factor.

This is the low-redshift form of the Hubble–Lemaître relation.

A larger redshift means light was emitted when the scale factor was smaller.

⚠️ Important distinctions

Expansion ≠ explosion

There is no established central point from which the universe is exploding into pre-existing empty space.

Recession ≠ ordinary local motion

For distant galaxies, cosmological recession is a result of evolving spacetime geometry. It should not be interpreted simply as local motion through static space.

Faster-than-light recession ≠ faster-than-light travel

At sufficiently large cosmological distances, recession rates can exceed c in a chosen cosmological description without violating special relativity's local speed limit for motion through spacetime.

Expansion ≠ objects growing

Bound systems are not simply stretched along with the cosmic scale factor.

❓ Common questions

Where is the center of the universe?

The standard homogeneous expansion model does not require a special center. Every sufficiently typical observer sees distant galaxies receding on large scales.

What is the universe expanding into?

General relativity describes the changing geometry of spacetime; the standard model does not require an external container or surrounding space into which the universe expands.

Are galaxies themselves expanding?

Not in the same way as the large-scale universe. Gravitationally bound structures can maintain their size while the space between unbound structures expands.

Why do distant galaxies look red?

Light traveling through expanding space can have its wavelength stretched, producing cosmological redshift. This is different from the redshift caused by an object's ordinary motion alone.

Is expansion faster than light?

Very distant galaxies can have a recession rate greater than c in cosmological coordinates. This does not mean an object is locally traveling through space faster than light.

Is the expansion rate constant?

No. The expansion history changes with cosmic time because different components of the universe affect the dynamics differently. The present expansion is accelerating.

What is the Hubble tension?

It is a persistent difference between some measurements of the present expansion rate using late-universe distance indicators and values inferred from early-universe observations within the standard cosmological model. The disagreement remains an active area of research.

⚛️ Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) is the period in the first few minutes of the hot early universe when nuclear reactions produced the first light atomic nuclei. It explains the primordial abundances of hydrogen isotopes, helium, and small amounts of lithium and provides a quantitative test of the hot Big Bang model.

Scientific status: BBN is a well-tested part of standard cosmology. Its predictions can be compared with primordial light-element abundances and with independent measurements of the baryon density from the cosmic microwave background.

🎬 Video

Original video is embedded through YouTube; CosmoLab does not download or re-upload it.

📖 What is it?

BBN began after the universe had cooled enough for protons and neutrons to participate in nuclear reactions, while temperatures were still billions of degrees. The reactions occurred roughly during the first few minutes, before expansion and cooling made further nuclear fusion inefficient.

The dominant primordial products were hydrogen and helium, including deuterium and helium-3, with a very small amount of lithium-7. Heavier elements were not produced in large quantities by BBN; most elements heavier than helium were made later inside stars and in stellar explosions and other energetic events.

⚙️ How does it work?

1. Protons and neutrons

The early universe contained a hot plasma of particles. As it expanded and cooled, free neutrons and protons became available for nuclear reactions.

2. Deuterium formation

Protons and neutrons could bind to form deuterium once energetic photons were no longer able to immediately break every newly formed deuterium nucleus apart.

3. Helium production

A network of nuclear reactions converted much of the available neutrons into helium-4. Small quantities of deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7 remained.

4. Reactions stop

Cosmic expansion lowered the temperature and density, so nuclear reaction rates became too low to keep building nuclei efficiently.

🔬 Evidence and tests

Helium-4

The observed primordial helium abundance is broadly consistent with hot-Big-Bang nucleosynthesis calculations.

Deuterium

Deuterium is especially useful because its primordial abundance is sensitive to the baryon density and can be compared with cosmological measurements.

Lithium-7

Lithium provides an important precision test, and the long-standing difference between some predicted and observed primordial lithium abundances remains an active research issue.

CMB cross-check

The baryon density inferred from CMB observations can be compared with the density required by BBN, providing an important consistency test of standard cosmology.

⏳ Timeline / development

First seconds

The universe was an extremely hot particle-radiation plasma, with nuclear reactions and weak interactions setting the conditions for later nucleosynthesis.

About 1–3 minutes

Deuterium formation and the subsequent nuclear reaction network produced most primordial helium and smaller abundances of other light nuclei.

Several minutes

Expansion and cooling reduced reaction rates, ending efficient primordial nucleosynthesis.

Later universe

Stars and other astrophysical processes became the main sites for producing elements heavier than the light primordial nuclei.

❓ Common questions

Did BBN make all the elements?

No. BBN mainly produced hydrogen, helium and trace amounts of a few light nuclei. Most heavier elements were produced much later by stars and other astrophysical processes.

Why did nucleosynthesis stop?

Expansion rapidly lowered the temperature and density, making nuclear reactions too slow to continue building nuclei efficiently.

Why is lithium important?

Primordial lithium measurements provide a sensitive test of BBN. Some observations show a persistent difference from standard BBN predictions, known as the cosmological lithium problem.

Why is BBN important?

It connects nuclear physics with cosmology and gives a quantitative prediction that can be checked against observations of primordial light elements.

📡 Cosmic Microwave Background

The cosmic microwave background (CMB) is the oldest light we can observe. It is relic radiation released when the universe became transparent about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, and its tiny temperature and polarization patterns preserve information about the early universe.

Scientific status: The CMB is directly observed and is one of the strongest observational foundations of modern cosmology.

🎬 Video

Original video is embedded through YouTube; CosmoLab does not download or re-upload it.

📖 What is it?

The early universe was a hot, dense plasma in which photons repeatedly scattered from free electrons. Around 380,000 years after the Big Bang, cooling allowed electrons to bind with nuclei to form mostly neutral atoms. Photons could then travel freely across space; those photons are observed today as the CMB.

Cosmic expansion has stretched the wavelengths of those ancient photons from much shorter wavelengths into the microwave range. Today the CMB has an average temperature of about 2.725 K.

⚙️ How does it work?

1. Photon scattering

Before recombination, free electrons made the universe opaque to light through frequent Thomson scattering.

2. Recombination

As the universe cooled, electrons combined with nuclei, greatly reducing the number of free electrons and allowing photons to decouple from matter.

3. Free-streaming photons

The released photons have traveled through the universe ever since, while cosmic expansion stretched their wavelengths.

4. Tiny fluctuations

Small temperature and polarization variations encode information about density perturbations, geometry and the contents of the early universe.

🔬 What does the CMB tell us?

🌌 Early-universe conditions

The CMB is a snapshot of the universe when it first became transparent, giving direct access to conditions long before stars and galaxies existed.

📐 Geometry

The angular pattern of CMB fluctuations strongly constrains the geometry of the universe on large scales.

⚛️ Matter and baryons

The fluctuation spectrum constrains the densities of ordinary matter, dark matter and radiation, as well as other cosmological parameters.

🌱 Seeds of structure

The small density variations seen in the CMB are connected to the fluctuations that gravity later amplified into galaxies and the cosmic web.

❓ Common questions

Is the CMB the Big Bang itself?

No. The CMB is radiation released much later, when the universe became transparent.

Why can we see the CMB from every direction?

We are surrounded by a spherical last-scattering surface: photons reaching us today come from many directions across the observable universe.

Can we see earlier than the CMB with light?

Not directly with ordinary electromagnetic light, because the earlier universe was opaque. Other messengers, such as neutrinos or gravitational waves, can in principle probe earlier epochs.

Why is the CMB so cold now?

Cosmic expansion stretched the photons' wavelengths, lowering the radiation temperature from its much hotter early value to about 2.725 K today.

What are CMB anisotropies?

They are tiny variations in the CMB temperature and polarization across the sky. They encode information about primordial fluctuations and cosmological parameters.

🌑 Dark Energy

Dark energy is the name given to the unknown component or physical effect associated with the accelerated expansion of the universe. It is not a substance that has been directly identified, and its fundamental nature remains one of the major open questions in cosmology.

Scientific status: The acceleration of cosmic expansion is observationally established, while the physical nature of dark energy is not. Several explanations remain under investigation.

🎬 Video

Original video is embedded through YouTube; CosmoLab does not download or re-upload it.

📖 What is it?

Observations show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating. In the standard cosmological model, this is described by a dark-energy component, often represented by the cosmological constant Λ.

Dark energy should not be confused with dark matter. Dark matter helps build gravitational structure, while dark energy is associated with the accelerated expansion of the universe. Neither term identifies a directly observed new substance in a laboratory.

🔭 How was it discovered?

Type Ia supernovae

In the late 1990s, observations of distant Type Ia supernovae showed that the universe's expansion history was not simply decelerating under gravity; the data indicated late-time acceleration.

Multiple probes

Supernovae, the CMB, baryon acoustic oscillations and large-scale structure are used together to constrain the expansion history and dark-energy behavior.

🧩 Leading possibilities

Λ / vacuum energy

The simplest standard description is a cosmological constant, corresponding to a constant energy density of the vacuum in the usual interpretation.

Dynamic dark energy

The cosmic acceleration could instead involve a field whose energy density changes over time, often discussed under models such as quintessence.

Modified gravity

Another possibility is that gravity behaves differently on cosmological scales, changing the expansion history without requiring dark energy to be a new component.

Unknown physics

The observed acceleration could ultimately require an explanation beyond the commonly studied models. Current evidence does not uniquely identify the underlying cause.

⏳ Timeline / development

1917

Einstein introduced the cosmological constant into general relativity while exploring a static cosmological model.

1920s

Evidence and theory shifted cosmology toward an expanding universe, changing how the cosmological constant was interpreted.

1998

Two independent supernova teams reported evidence for accelerating cosmic expansion.

2000s–today

CMB, supernovae, BAO, weak lensing and galaxy surveys have provided increasingly precise constraints on the expansion history.

🧮 Key mathematics

Friedmann acceleration equation

ä/a = −(4πG/3)(ρ + 3p/c²)

Cosmic acceleration occurs when the effective combination of energy density and pressure makes the right-hand side positive.

Equation of state

w = p/(ρc²)

For a cosmological constant, the effective equation-of-state parameter is approximately w = −1.

Λ in ΛCDM

ΛCDM

The standard model combines a cosmological constant with cold dark matter, ordinary matter, radiation and other components.

Density parameter

ΩΛ = ρΛ/ρcrit

This expresses the dark-energy density relative to the critical density in a chosen cosmological model.

📊 What we know — and what we don't

Known

The universe's expansion is accelerating, based on multiple independent cosmological observations.

Unknown

We do not yet know whether dark energy is vacuum energy, a dynamic field, modified gravity, or something else.

Model dependence

Statements about the exact fraction of the universe attributed to dark energy depend on the cosmological model and data combination used.

Active research

Large surveys and observatories continue testing whether the expansion history is fully consistent with a constant cosmological term.

❓ Common questions

Is dark energy a real substance?

We know there is an effect associated with accelerated expansion, but the underlying physical entity has not been directly identified.

Is dark energy the same as dark matter?

No. They play different roles in cosmological models: dark matter contributes gravitational attraction and structure formation, while dark energy is associated with late-time accelerated expansion.

Could dark energy change?

Possibly. Some models allow a time-varying dark-energy component, while the simplest ΛCDM model uses a constant cosmological term. Observations continue to test these possibilities.

What happens to the universe in the future?

The long-term fate depends on the physics governing cosmic acceleration. Different models can lead to different future expansion histories, so this remains a scientific question rather than a settled prediction.

            Uncertainty principle

            Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2
              —
              minimum momentum uncertainty

              Pinning down a particle's position more precisely necessarily blurs how precisely its momentum can be known — this is a property of nature, not a limit of measurement equipment.
